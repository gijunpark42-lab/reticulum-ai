On Tuesday, 18 August 2026, Applied Optoelectronics (AAOI) used Rosenblatt’s 6th Annual Technology Summit: The Age of AI (Part II) to outline a business being pulled in two directions. Demand for data center optics tied to artificial intelligence is running ahead of supply, but near-term margins are under pressure from higher costs, expedite fees and a drop in its high-margin 100G business.

Chief Financial Officer and Chief Strategy Officer Stefan Murry said the company&rsquo;s growth is now limited more by manufacturing capacity than by customer interest. He also argued that AOI&rsquo;s U.S.-based laser production, automation and proprietary technology give it an edge as customers look for more secure supply chains.

AOI said recent profitability has been squeezed by several temporary and structural factors:

Management lowered its near-term gross margin target to the low-to-mid 30% range, or about 32% to 33%, by the end of 2024. That is below earlier expectations of 35%. The longer-term target remains 40% by the end of 2027, with the possibility of margins moving above that level in 2028 if CPO and NPO products become meaningful revenue contributors. The company’s gross profit margin stood at roughly 29% over the last twelve months, reflecting the current pressure from expedite costs and product mix shifts. According to InvestingPro analysis, AAOI currently appears overvalued relative to its Fair Value, placing it among stocks on the platform’s Most Overvalued list&mdash;though analysts predict the company will return to profitability this year as capacity expansions take hold.

The company also said capital spending has remained heavy. First-half 2024 capex was significant, and second-half spending is expected to be at least as large. Management said the return on current investments is about 9 to 10 months, with most spending going to production equipment, machinery and real estate rather than research and development.

AOI described a rapid buildout across its manufacturing base, with a focus on transceivers and lasers for data centers.

Production and capacity:

Laser manufacturing and technology:

On the transceiver side, management said the company&rsquo;s production process is highly automated and flexible enough to switch among LRO, LPO and full DSP solutions, depending on customer demand and supply chain conditions. LRO products are already productized, while LPO designs exist but are not fully productized. Customers are mainly asking for full DSP solutions today.

AOI said demand is strong across its data center optics portfolio, especially in 800G and 1.6T products.

Revenue outlook by product:

AOI said two major hyperscale customers are driving 800G volume, with several smaller customers contributing smaller amounts. For 1.6T, one customer has already placed a $200 million order, and at least one more major customer is in qualification trials. The company’s strong execution has driven remarkable investor returns, with the stock delivering a 493% gain over the past year and climbing 344% year-to-date, according to InvestingPro data. Management said it is effectively sold out through the second half of 2025 and cannot easily take on another large data center customer right now. It said the issue is not demand, but the ability to build enough capacity without overpromising to existing customers.

AOI also said it is in active CPO discussions with five companies, some of which are also talking about NPO. The company expects multiple CPO customers by 2028, though it does not expect to win all five because of capacity limits.

Murry framed the AI infrastructure buildout as the biggest opportunity the optics industry has seen in decades. He said scale-up applications could require an order-of-magnitude increase in laser content per rack compared with today&rsquo;s scale-out systems, along with much larger die sizes and far more wafer processing.

He also said AOI&rsquo;s long-term market share could rise to about 20% in data center transceivers by 2025 and 2026, though he did not describe that as a dominant position. Larger competitors will still exist, he said, but not all of them will be based in the U.S.

Management stressed that the company&rsquo;s growth path is tied to capital spending. If AOI does not invest, it risks becoming too small to matter, Murry said. The company said it is funding expansion mainly through equity for now, but it is also considering other sources:

AOI said it has not historically relied on mergers and acquisitions to expand capacity or add intellectual property. Management said its organic growth model fits its proprietary production process better than buying another company and trying to integrate different manufacturing methods.

AOI said its lasers are comparable or superior in design and specifications to those of Lumentum, Coherent and Broadcom, especially at higher power levels such as 300 to 400 milliwatts. Management said the main difference is not technical capability, but the ability to manufacture at scale.

The company said U.S.-based production is becoming more important to customers because of geopolitical concerns, including the risk of Chinese import restrictions. Management said customers are willing to pay a premium for supply chain security.
